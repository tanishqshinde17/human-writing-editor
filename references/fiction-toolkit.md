# Fiction Toolkit

Load this file in deep narrative review mode: long fiction, a major revision, or a request for detailed critique. For a short scene or a light revision, the lenses in SKILL.md section 4 are usually enough; use single tools from here (the scene card, the dialogue diagnostic) only when they help.

All SKILL.md rules still apply here, especially: no invented facts, explicit user instructions win, and the watch list is a set of questions, not a recipe.

## 1. Deep review procedure

Work in passes. Each pass looks for one kind of problem, so later passes do not polish text that an earlier pass will cut.

**Pass A: Purpose.** Read the whole piece once without editing. Write privately, in one sentence each: the premise; the protagonist's want; the main pressure against it; what has changed by the end. If any of these cannot be written, that is the first finding.

**Pass B: Structure.** List the scenes or sections in one line each, with the scene card's "function" for each (section 2). Flag any scene whose removal would not change the outcome or the reader's understanding. Check proportion: does time on the page match importance?

**Pass C: Character and agency.** For each major character: want, fear, what they know, what they misread. Are they distinguishable under pressure? Does anyone exist only to deliver information?

**Pass D: Relationships.** Who holds leverage, and does that shift? Do absent people, family or institutions press on the action? Does any relationship end exactly where it began without that being the point?

**Pass E: Revelation and time.** Map what the reader knows at each stage. Are surprises earned, and do they change how earlier scenes read? Is the chronology serving the revelations, or just defaulting to start-to-finish?

**Pass F: Point of view, voice and dialogue.** Check POV stability and narrative distance. Run the dialogue diagnostic (section 3) on the three or four most important exchanges.

**Pass G: Line level and continuity.** Apply SKILL.md section 5 to a representative sample, not every line. Then check the continuity sheet (section 7).

**Report format.**
1. A two or three sentence verdict: what the story is doing and whether it works.
2. The three most important issues, ranked by impact on the whole piece, each with where it shows and a concrete direction.
3. Scene-level notes for the scenes that need them.
4. A few line-level examples showing the pattern, not a full line edit.
5. What is working and should be protected in revision.

Do not rewrite the whole story unless asked. If asked, revise in sections and confirm direction after the first.

## 2. Scene card

Fill this privately for any scene that feels flat or unclear.

- **Entry state:** where things stand when the scene opens.
- **Aim:** what the point-of-view character wants in this scene.
- **Resistance:** who or what opposes it.
- **Tactic:** what the character tries.
- **Turn:** the moment the situation changes, for better or worse.
- **Exit state:** where things stand now. If it equals the entry state, the scene has not moved.
- **Function:** what the scene does for the story (raises stakes, reveals, complicates a relationship, sets up a later payoff).

A scene can be quiet and still turn: a decision made, a suspicion confirmed, a relationship shifted by a degree.

## 3. Dialogue diagnostic

For each important exchange:

1. **Action.** What is each line doing: asking, refusing, testing, persuading, threatening, comforting, deflecting? A line that only delivers information is a candidate for narration or a cut.
2. **Want versus words.** What does the speaker want, and does the line say it directly? A gap between the two creates subtext; no gap is fine when directness is the character.
3. **Status.** Who gains or loses ground across the exchange?
4. **Voice.** Could another character say this line unchanged? If so, the voices need distinguishing through vocabulary, rhythm, what each notices, or what each avoids.

Also check for "as you know" exposition, characters explaining the theme to each other, and dialogue tags that tell the reader how to read a line the line already shows.

## 4. Opening checks

- Does the opening give the reader someone to follow and something at stake, or a reason to keep reading, within the first page?
- Is the reader grounded enough (who, where, roughly when) to follow, without a block of backstory first?
- Does the first line earn its place, or is it weather, waking up or a generic reflection that could open any story?
- Does the opening promise the kind of story that follows (genre, tone, scale)?

## 5. Ending checks

- Does the ending answer the story's central question, even if the answer is "no" or "not yet"?
- Is the resolution caused by things the story established, rather than luck, a new character or a sudden realization?
- Does the final image or line add something, or restate the theme?
- Has the cost been paid? Something should be different, and usually something should be lost.
- Is there an epilogue or extended denouement? Keep it only if it changes how the ending reads.

## 6. Subgenre weighting

Weight the lenses by genre. These are emphases, not rules.

- **Mystery and detective:** fair-play clues, information control, a solution that recontextualizes earlier scenes. Check that the detective's reasoning is on the page.
- **Thriller and suspense:** escalation that raises real cost, a clock, and reversals caused by the antagonist's choices, not coincidence.
- **Romance:** both leads with wants of their own, an obstacle rooted in character rather than a single misunderstanding, and a resolution the relationship has earned.
- **Horror:** dread built by what is withheld, a rule or threat that stays consistent, and atmosphere that changes what characters do.
- **Fantasy and science fiction:** worldbuilding delivered through action and consequence, rules that constrain the ending, and invented terms kept to what the reader needs.
- **Literary:** interiority with movement, a change that may be small but is real, and ambiguity that is chosen rather than accidental.
- **Comedy and humour:** timing, escalation of the comic premise, and characters who take their situation seriously. Never explain a joke.
- **Children's and young adult:** age-appropriate clarity, a protagonist who acts rather than being rescued, and explicit emotional naming where it helps young readers.

## 7. Continuity sheet

Keep this for long fiction, or any piece revised across several messages:

- Character names, spellings, ages, relationships and physical details mentioned.
- Place names and geography.
- Timeline: dates, days of the week, seasons, elapsed time.
- Tense and point of view by section.
- Invented terms, titles and rules of the world.
- Style decisions: spelling variety, punctuation preferences, dialogue formatting.

Check each revised section against the sheet before delivery.

## 8. Where the research watch list fits

The watch list lives in SKILL.md section 4.0 and is not repeated here. In a deep review, ask each of its questions once, in the pass where it belongs:

- **Stated themes:** Pass A (does the purpose sentence match what the narrator says the story means?) and Pass F (does any character argue the theme like an essay?).
- **One tidy causal chain** and **resolution by inner acceptance:** Pass B and the ending checks in section 5.
- **Morally tidy protagonists:** Pass C.
- **Strict chronology and shallow surprises:** Pass E.
- **Vague allusion** and direct reader address: Pass F.
- **Emotion carried by the body and the senses:** Pass G, on the line-level sample.

Report a watch-list item only when it weakens this particular story. It is never a finding on its own.
